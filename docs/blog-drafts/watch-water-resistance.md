A watch's water-resistance marking tells you about a pressure rating; it does not promise that you can wear the watch to the stated depth in everyday conditions. For any activity, check the instructions for your exact model. If there is no rating or you cannot verify its condition, keep the watch dry until a qualified watchmaker has checked it.

## What does a watch water-resistance rating mean?

Manufacturers usually express a rating in metres, bar or atmospheres (ATM). The number describes a test pressure, not a simple instruction to swim or dive to that depth. Movement through water, temperature changes, the condition of seals and how the crown is secured can affect real-world use. A watch is **water resistant**, not permanently waterproof.

The rating may be printed on the dial or case back, or listed in the instructions. Look for the exact model number as well: watches that look similar may have different ratings. The [Timex guide to water-resistance ratings](https://timex.com/pages/water-resistance-ratings) explains common markings for its own watches; it should not replace the instructions supplied with another manufacturer's model.

## Can I swim with a 30m or 50m watch?

Do not assume that a 30m marking permits swimming. Manufacturers commonly describe a 30m or 3 bar watch as suitable for limited everyday splashes, not immersion. If it gets wet, follow the maker's care instructions and dry it with a soft cloth.

A 50m or 5 bar marking is also not a universal permission slip. Some manufacturers allow swimming for particular 50m watches, while others recommend a more conservative approach. For example, an [Epson watch manual](https://mediaserver.goepson.com/ImConvServlet/imconv/b45c10a5af325146232e4b8815c184948bf26d8a/original?assetDescr=Manual_AM-HH_EMAM72.pdf) describes swimming for its reinforced 50m category. That instruction belongs to the watches covered by the manual; check your own model before entering the pool.

The same principle applies at 100m and above. A higher rating can indicate a watch is intended for more demanding use, but the exact permitted activities, crown requirements and service schedule come from its manufacturer. A rating alone does not make a watch suitable for scuba diving. For diving, use equipment specifically designed and approved for the intended activity and follow its instructions.

| Marking | How to interpret it safely |
| --- | --- |
| No stated rating | Keep dry unless the manufacturer confirms otherwise. |
| 30m / 3 bar | Generally limited splash protection; do not assume swimming is permitted. |
| 50m / 5 bar | Check the exact model's swimming guidance before immersion. |
| 100m / 10 bar or higher | Check the model's instructions and condition; do not infer diving suitability from the number alone. |

## Where should I check the rating?

1. **Find the model reference.** Look on the case back, paperwork or product page.
2. **Read the exact marking.** Note the unit as well as the number; do not rely on an advertisement's general wording.
3. **Find the manufacturer's instructions.** Look for its activity chart, crown instructions and care recommendations.
4. **Consider the watch's condition.** A rating applies to a watch in suitable condition; an older watch or one with a damaged crystal, loose crown or unknown service history needs attention.
5. **Ask when in doubt.** A qualified watchmaker can inspect the watch and advise whether a pressure test is appropriate.

If you are comparing models, use the available specifications on the [Timect watch collection](/watches) and ask about the exact watch rather than assuming every model has the same rating.

## Why can water resistance change over time?

Gaskets are seals used around openings such as the case back and crown. They can age, and knocks or servicing can affect how the case seals. [Hamilton's care guidance](https://www.hamiltonwatch.com/en-int/faq) notes that water resistance is not permanent and can be affected by ageing gaskets or accidental impacts.

Battery changes, repairs and opening the case are good moments to ask a qualified service provider whether the seals need attention and whether the watch should be tested. Do not assume that an intact-looking watch has passed a pressure test. Ask the manufacturer or service centre how often testing is recommended for your model and how you use it.

## What about the crown, buttons and strap?

Before any permitted water exposure, make sure the crown is fully seated and, if the watch has a screw-down crown, secured according to its instructions. Avoid operating the crown in water. Button guidance differs by model; [Timex](https://timex.com/pages/water-resistance-ratings), for example, gives specific instructions about underwater button use for its watches. When in doubt, leave buttons alone.

The case rating and the strap's suitability are separate questions. Leather can be affected by moisture and is not a good choice for repeated soaking even if the watch case is rated for water. After ordinary wear, use the strap maker's instructions; [Skagen recommends a soft microfibre cloth for surface cleaning leather](https://www.skagen.com/en-gb/customer-care/watch-instructions/). Our [strap guide](/blog/how-to-choose-the-right-watch-strap) explains material choices.

Hot showers, steam rooms and sudden temperature changes are best avoided unless the manufacturer explicitly permits them. Heat and chemicals are different challenges from the pressure test behind a rating. Similarly, do not clean a watch under a tap just because it has a number on the case back: check the model instructions first.

## What should I do if there is moisture under the crystal?

If droplets appear inside the watch, or mist persists or returns, keep the watch away from water and contact a qualified watchmaker promptly. Avoid heating it with a hair dryer or opening the case yourself. Describe when the moisture appeared and whether the watch has been immersed, knocked or recently serviced. A professional can assess the cause and the condition of the seals.

## A practical rule before you get wet

Read the marking, find the instructions for the exact model, and consider the condition of the watch and its strap. If any of those are uncertain, keep it dry and ask for advice. Choosing a suitable watch starts with what you actually plan to do while wearing it—not just the largest number on the case back.
